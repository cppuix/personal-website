<p>"We have two hundred thousand subscribers".<br />"That clip got half a million views".<br />"Imagine how many people received that benefit."</p>
<p>And the feeling behind it is genuine. The hope is real. The desire to spread good is not in question.</p>
<p>But the number is being used to answer a question it was never designed to answer.</p>
<p>Not: <i>how many people saw it.</i></p>
<p>But: <i>what happened to them after.</i></p>There is a difference between information reaching a person and formation happening inside one.<p>Information is transferred. Formation is grown.</p>
<p>Information can happen in seconds — a clip, a quote, a reminder that lands and feels true in the moment.</p>
<p>Formation takes longer than that. Much longer.</p>
<p>It requires return. It requires repetition that isn't passive. It requires something sitting in a person long enough to change the shape of how they think, how they speak, how they move through the world.</p>
<p>You cannot scroll your way into that.</p>The reach argument quietly breaks down because it assumes that reaching someone is the same as affecting them, exposure is equivalent to impact, and that a view is a form of benefit.<p>But a benefit is not just content that feels true in the moment, it is something that meets a person where they actually are, in their practical life, in what they are currently living through, currently struggling with, currently needing to act on.</p>
<p>Something that has no ground to land in passes through. It might feel good. It might even feel true. But if it speaks to no real situation, no actual gap in someone's practice, no struggle they are already inside — it does not settle. It moves on. And they move on with it, to the next thing.</p>
<p>This is what Sufyān ath-Thawrī's mother understood. She did not ask her son how much he had written. She asked whether it showed — in how he walked, in his forbearance, in his composure. That is a question about landing, not arrival.</p>
<p>Al-Albānī, when asked how a student of knowledge should begin, said: by priority. What is most obligatory and relevant to him, in his life, right now. He didn't suggest what is impressive or "interesting", but what he actually needs.</p><p>Real daʿwah works the same way. It is not a broadcast. It is an address — to a person, in a condition, with a need.</p><p>The Prophet ﷺ had proximity, not scale, not optimized content, and definitely not a platform.</p>
<p>He had people who sat with him, watched how he moved, heard how he said things, not just what he said. They were shaped by nearness — slowly, repeatedly, over years.</p>
<p>The Companions did not learn Islam comprehensively before they lived it. They learned ten āyāt. They stopped. They worked with those ten until they became practice. Then they took more.</p>
<p>That is not just a learning style, it is an understanding of how formation works: you can only absorb what you have soil for. And the soil is your current life, your current need, your current condition.</p><p>Those people changed civilizations not because of how many heard them, but because of what was actually in them — and how it transmitted.</p><p>A thousand people who carry something are more powerful than a million who consumed it.</p><p>Because the ones who carry it transmit it differently. With weight. The way the Companions transmitted it — not as information passed along, but as something they themselves were shaped by.</p><p>The bearer of musk does not need a large following. They just need to be near people.</p><p>None of this means platforms are worthless. They are not.</p><p>I know you might reach that conclusion, because the human nature is quick to reach extreme ends.</p><p>But, what that means is that the metric is wrong.</p>
<p>When we measure daʿwah by how many people saw something, we are measuring the arrival, not the landing.</p>
<p>The better question — the more honest question — is:</p>
<p><i>Did it settle in anyone?</i></p>
<p>Did it meet someone where they actually were? Did it speak to something they were already living through? Did it find its way, quietly, into the fabric of how they act?</p>
<p>That is what daʿwah is supposed to produce.</p>
<p>Not numbers. Settlement.</p><p>Not whether someone's device registered its view because the pixels on its screen displayed the content.</p><p>So, as I like to do, rant, then propose a solution, which is an invitation — to the one creating, and to the one consuming:</p><p>Stop measuring in views. Start measuring in return.</p>
<p>Did they come back?
Did it change how they acted on something?
Did it sit with them through the week?</p>
<p>If yes — something real happened, regardless of the number.</p>
<p>If no — the reach was real. But the daʿwah wasn't.</p>
<p>And daʿwah, not distribution, is the whole point.</p>
<p>That has always been the whole point.</p><p>We just fell into the trap of social media. A system made by the kuffār to kill attention spans and promote fast-paced content.</p>
