<p><span style="font-family: IBM Plex Sans Arabic;"><b>1. Strong command of the source language</b><br />Not the textbook type. The lived, intuitive grasp of idioms, registers, rhetorical structures, implied meanings, and cultural assumptions.<br /><br /><br /><b>2. Strong command of the target language</b><br />Same as the above; listed separately to emphasize its importance.<br /><br /><br /><b>3. Grounded knowledge in Islamic sciences</b><br />This is often the most missing. True translation of religious material demands expertise in the field, because one unfamiliar with it cannot convey its meaning.<br />Someone like that is more of a bilingual tourist than a translator of Islamic texts.<br /><br /><br /><b>4. Competence in resolving meaning</b><br />The actual craft. Understanding the author’s intent, distinguishing between literal and figurative, spotting ellipses, identifying probable interpretations when the text allows for more than one.<br /><br /><br /><b>5. Competence in reconstructing meaning</b><br />* Don't let your style, assumptions, or shortcuts change it.<br />* Don’t generalize, simplify, or paraphrase in a way that loses nuance.<br />* Don’t allow the translation to gradually deviate from the original sense.<br /></span></p><p><span style="font-family: IBM Plex Sans Arabic;"><br /><b>6. Honesty, Humility, and Restraint</b><br />A translator who cannot say "I don’t know" is a liability.<br />Details:<br />* Knowing when a term, idiom, or doctrinal nuance is beyond your certainty. This requires realizing what you don't know, which is half of knowledge itself.<br />* Resisting the urge to "fill in gaps" with assumptions or educated guesses. This usually stems from underestimating the seriousness of the text.<br /><br />These conditions are the goal; translators vary in their ability to meet them.<br />A clear outline, even if general, helps maintain calibration.</span></p>
